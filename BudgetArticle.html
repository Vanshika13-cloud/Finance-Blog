Budgeting

Build a Budget You'll Actually Stick With

Forget the 47-tab spreadsheet. Three categories, one rule, and ten minutes a week is all it really takes.

Budgeting · No spreadsheet required

Somewhere out there is a spreadsheet with 47 tabs, color-coded categories for "miscellaneous miscellaneous," and a formula error that's been silently wrong since March. If that's your idea of budgeting, no wonder it never stuck. A budget doesn't need to track everything perfectly — it needs to be simple enough that you'll actually keep doing it in month four, not just week one.

01 — Why Budgets FailIt's not a willpower problem

Most budgets don't fail because people lack discipline. They fail because the system was too complicated to maintain. Twenty categories, manual entry for every purchase, an app you forget to open — eventually life gets busy and the whole thing quietly dies. The fix isn't more discipline. It's a system with so little friction that skipping it feels harder than doing it.

02 — Three CategoriesAll you actually need to track

Forget granular categories for coffee, rideshares, and streaming subscriptions separately. Almost everything you spend fits into three buckets:

50%
Needs
Rent, groceries, utilities, minimum debt payments
30%
Wants
Dinners out, travel, subscriptions, hobbies
20%
Future You
Savings, investing, extra debt payoff

These percentages are a starting point, not a law. If rent eats 45% of your income on its own, that's real — the other buckets just flex around it. What matters is having three honest totals instead of zero.

Reframe it

You're not tracking every latte. You're tracking three numbers, once a week, so you always know roughly where you stand.

03 — One RuleAutomate before you can talk yourself out of it

The single highest-leverage move in budgeting is paying your "Future You" bucket automatically, on payday, before you ever see the money in your checking account. Willpower is a limited resource by the end of a long week — automation doesn't get tired.

Set up a recurring transfer to a savings or investing account for the day after payday. Whatever's left in checking is what you actually have to work with for needs and wants. You stop having to decide to save every single time, because the decision only had to be made once.

The one rule, restated

Pay Future You first, automatically. Everything else can be a little messy and it'll still work.

04 — Ten Minutes a WeekThe only maintenance this needs

Once a week — Sunday night, payday morning, whenever it'll actually happen — spend ten minutes glancing at your accounts. You're not building a report. You're just checking three things:

  • Did the automatic transfer go through? Confirm Future You got paid.
  • Does anything look off? A surprise charge, a subscription you forgot about, a bill due sooner than expected.
  • Roughly, how do the buckets feel? Not exact math — just a gut check on whether wants spending is creeping up.

Ten minutes a week is under nine hours a year. That's the entire maintenance cost of a budget that actually works.

If a week gets missed

Skip the guilt and just do next week's check-in. A budget you return to after missing a week still works fine. A budget you abandon after one missed week doesn't.

05 — Common BreakersWhat actually derails this

  • Irregular income: budget off your lowest typical month, and treat anything above that as a bonus to split between buckets.
  • One big expense breaking the month: that's normal — let the Wants bucket absorb it and rebalance the following week rather than abandoning the system.
  • Shared expenses with a partner or roommate: agree on one shared "Needs" number up front so it isn't renegotiated every month.

This week, do these three things

1

Split last month into three buckets

Roughly sort where your money went — Needs, Wants, Future You — just to see your real starting point.

2

Automate one transfer

Set a recurring transfer to savings or investing for the day after payday, even if it's small.

3

Pick your ten minutes

Choose a specific day and time for your weekly check-in, and put it on a recurring calendar reminder.

A budget that's 80% accurate and takes ten minutes a week beats a perfect one that takes two hours and gets abandoned by March. Simple isn't the lazy option here — it's the one that actually survives contact with real life.
